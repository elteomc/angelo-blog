The R-transform turns free addition into ordinary addition

Angelo Farfan

We know that the free sum of two symmetric sign variables has second moment two and fourth moment six. What is its entire distribution?

Part II supplied an additive sequence of numbers: the free cumulants. Packaging those numbers into a function lets us solve this question with a quadratic equation and a careful choice of square root.

The Cauchy transform

For a compactly supported probability measure \(\mu\) on \(\mathbb R\), define its Cauchy transform by \[ G_\mu(z)=\int\frac{1}{z-x}\,d\mu(x),\qquad \operatorname{Im}z>0. \] Our sign convention matters. With \(1/(z-x)\), the imaginary part of \(G\) is negative in the upper half-plane. Some references use the negative of this transform.

For sufficiently large \(|z|\), expand the geometric series: \[ G_\mu(z)=\frac1z+\frac{m_1}{z^2}+\frac{m_2}{z^3}+\cdots. \] Thus \(G\) encodes every moment, and its leading behavior must be \(1/z\).

It also recovers the density. Indeed, \[ -\frac1\pi\operatorname{Im}G_\mu(x+i\eta) =\int\frac{\eta}{\pi((x-t)^2+\eta^2)}\,d\mu(t). \] The right side smooths the measure with a Cauchy kernel. At continuity points of a density \(\rho\), letting \(\eta\downarrow0\) gives \(\rho(x)\). More generally, Stieltjes inversion recovers masses of intervals. An atom at \(x_0\) can be checked separately through \[ \mu(\{x_0\})=\lim_{\eta\downarrow0}i\eta G_\mu(x_0+i\eta). \]

From the moment formula to a functional equation

Introduce the two generating series \[ M(t)=1+\sum_{n\geq1}m_nt^n, \qquad R(w)=\sum_{n\geq1}\kappa_nw^{n-1}. \] Look at the block containing position 1 in a noncrossing partition. If that block has size \(k\), it leaves \(k\) ordered gaps, each carrying an independent noncrossing partition. This decomposes the moment formula into \[ M(t)=1+\sum_{k\geq1}\kappa_k t^kM(t)^k =1+tM(t)R(tM(t)). \] Since \(G(z)=z^{-1}M(z^{-1})\), substitution gives \[ zG(z)=1+G(z)R(G(z)), \] or \[ \boxed{z=\frac1{G(z)}+R(G(z)).} \] Initially this identity holds near infinity, or as a formal Laurent-series identity. We should not assume that the power series for \(R\) converges at every value of \(G\).

Equivalently, let \(K\) denote the local compositional inverse of \(G\) near infinity, so \(K(w)\sim1/w\) near zero. Then \[ R(w)=K(w)-\frac1w. \] This is the R-transform. Cumulant additivity becomes \[ \boxed{R_{\mu\boxplus\nu}(w)=R_\mu(w)+R_\nu(w).} \] The local inverse and analytic extension are developed rigorously in Speicher, Theorem 4.12.

Warm-up: adding semicircles

A centered semicircular variable of variance \(v\) has only one nonzero cumulant, \(\kappa_2=v\). Therefore \(R(w)=vw\) and \[ vG(z)^2-zG(z)+1=0. \] For \(v>0\), choose the solution \[ G(z)=\frac{z-\sqrt{z^2-4v}}{2v}, \] where the square root is the branch asymptotic to \(z\) at infinity. The other solution grows like \(z/v\), which cannot be the transform of a probability measure.

Taking the boundary value in the upper half-plane recovers the semicircle density. Adding free semicircles of variances \(v_1,v_2\) simply replaces \(vw\) by \((v_1+v_2)w\). Their sum is semicircular with variance \(v_1+v_2\).

Adding two free sign variables

Let \(\mu=\tfrac12\delta_{-1}+\tfrac12\delta_1\). Its transform is immediate: \[ G_\mu(z)=\frac12\left(\frac1{z-1}+\frac1{z+1}\right) =\frac{z}{z^2-1}. \] Solve \(w=z/(z^2-1)\) for \(z\): \[ z=\frac{1\pm\sqrt{1+4w^2}}{2w}. \] The inverse with a \(1/w\) pole uses the plus sign, with the square root analytic and equal to one at \(w=0\). Hence \[ R_\mu(w)=\frac{\sqrt{1+4w^2}-1}{2w}. \] Expanding gives \(R_\mu(w)=w-w^3+2w^5-\cdots\), consistent with \(\kappa_2=1\) and \(\kappa_4=-1\).

For \(\nu=\mu\boxplus\mu\), add the two R-transforms. The equation for \(G_\nu\) becomes \[ z=\frac1{G_\nu(z)}+ \frac{\sqrt{1+4G_\nu(z)^2}-1}{G_\nu(z)} =\frac{\sqrt{1+4G_\nu(z)^2}}{G_\nu(z)}. \] Squaring yields \((z^2-4)G_\nu(z)^2=1\). Choose the branch matching \(G_\nu(z)\sim1/z\): \[ \boxed{G_\nu(z)=\frac1{\sqrt{z^2-4}}.} \] Here \(\sqrt{z^2-4}\) means the analytic branch on \(\mathbb C\setminus[-2,2]\) asymptotic to \(z\). It is not the pointwise principal square root of \(z^2-4\) everywhere. The formula agrees with the transform near infinity and continues analytically to the upper half-plane.

For \(-2<x<2\), its boundary value is \[ G_\nu(x+i0)=-\frac{i}{\sqrt{4-x^2}}. \] Therefore the answer is the arcsine law: \[ \boxed{d\nu(x)=\frac{\mathbf1_{(-2,2)}(x)}{\pi\sqrt{4-x^2}}\,dx.} \] The density diverges at the endpoints, but its integral is one. These integrable divergences are not atoms: multiplying the transform by \(i\eta\) at either endpoint gives zero in the limit.

Checking the moments

Set \(x=2\cos\theta\). The arcsine measure becomes uniform \(d\theta/\pi\) on \([0,\pi]\), so \[ \int x^2\,d\nu(x)=2,\qquad \int x^4\,d\nu(x)=6. \] More generally, its odd moments vanish and its \(2k\)th moment is \(\binom{2k}{k}\). This agrees with our mixed-word calculations.

Two free sign variables produce an arcsine law. Many free sign variables, summed and divided by the square root of their number, approach a semicircle. These are different questions, so there is no conflict between the two answers.

Exercises

  1. Find \(R_{\delta_c}\).
  2. Derive the transform rule for \(ca\) from cumulant scaling.
  3. Why is solving a polynomial equation for \(G\) insufficient without a branch condition?
Answer sketches
  1. \(G(z)=1/(z-c)\), so \(K(w)=c+1/w\) and \(R(w)=c\). Free addition of a constant translates the law.
  2. \(R_{ca}(w)=cR_a(cw)\), since the \(n\)th cumulant scales by \(c^n\).
  3. A polynomial may have several solutions. A probability Cauchy transform must be analytic in the upper half-plane, have negative imaginary part there, and behave like \(1/z\) at infinity. Squaring can introduce extraneous branches.

Further reading

Speicher’s lecture notes, Section 4 contain the analytic R-transform theory and the symmetric two-point example. Tao’s free probability notes give another route from transforms to free convolution. Check the sign convention when moving between references.
