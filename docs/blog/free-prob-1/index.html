Why eigenvalues alone do not tell us the spectrum of a sum

Angelo Farfan

Suppose \(A_n\) and \(B_n\) are two independent \(n\times n\) Hermitian random matrices. We understand the eigenvalues of each matrix separately. What should we expect from \(A_n+B_n\)?

For ordinary independent random variables, the two distributions determine the distribution of the sum. Matrices make the question more interesting: their eigenvectors have a relative geometry, and that geometry affects addition.

Free probability gives us a way to calculate when those eigenvectors are sufficiently mixed. Independence of the random matrices, on its own, will not be enough.

Eigenvalues are not enough

Take \[ A=B_1=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad B_2=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}. \] Both choices of \(B\) have spectrum \(\{-1,1\}\). Nevertheless, \[ \operatorname{spec}(A+B_1)=\{-2,2\},\qquad \operatorname{spec}(A+B_2)=\{0,0\}. \] The individual spectra have lost the information about which directions line up.

Writing \(A=U\Lambda U^*\) and \(B=V\Gamma V^*\) makes the missing ingredient explicit: the spectrum of their sum depends on the relative basis \(U^*V\).

Here is a useful replacement for choosing an alignment. Keep deterministic diagonal matrices \(D_n,E_n\), draw a Haar unitary \(U_n\), and study \[ D_n+U_nE_nU_n^*. \] Haar measure is the uniform probability measure on the unitary group, in the precise sense of invariance under multiplication by any fixed unitary. Conjugation preserves the spectrum of \(E_n\) and randomizes its eigenbasis.

Under suitable hypotheses, this produces a limiting rule for mixed moments. That rule is freeness.

The trace turns a matrix into a distribution

Define the empirical spectral measure and normalized trace by \[ \mu_A=\frac1n\sum_{j=1}^n\delta_{\lambda_j(A)}, \qquad \tau_n(A)=\frac1n\operatorname{Tr}(A). \] Then \[ \boxed{\tau_n(A^k)=\int x^k\,d\mu_A(x).} \] A normalized trace of a power is exactly a spectral moment. The normalization gives total mass one, so moments remain comparable as the dimension changes.

To study the sum, expand \(\tau_n((A+B)^k)\). Now words such as \(AABB\) and \(ABAB\) appear. They cannot generally be rearranged into each other. Trace is cyclic, so \(\tau(ABBA)=\tau(AABB)\), but it does not permit arbitrary permutations.

For a random matrix, \(\tau_n(A^k)\) is itself random. Its ensemble average is \(\mathbb E[\tau_n(A^k)]\). We will distinguish these when discussing experiments and convergence.

Noncommutative probability

Our setting is a unital algebra \(\mathcal A\) with an adjoint operation and a normalized positive trace \(\tau\). Thus \(\tau\) is linear and satisfies \[ \tau(1)=1,\qquad \tau(x^*x)\geq0,\qquad \tau(xy)=\tau(yx). \] Matrices with normalized trace are the finite-dimensional example. For bounded self-adjoint operators, the spectral theorem gives a probability law whose moments are \(\tau(a^k)\). We use compactly supported laws throughout the series, so their moments determine them uniquely.

Think of a self-adjoint element \(a=a^*\) as a real random variable and \(\tau\) as expectation. Multiplication may fail to commute, but positivity and spectral measures remain available.

Freeness is a rule for centered, alternating products

Unital subalgebras \(\mathcal A_1,\mathcal A_2,\ldots\) are free if \[ \tau(x_1x_2\cdots x_m)=0 \] whenever each \(x_i\) belongs to \(\mathcal A_{j_i}\), consecutive indices differ, and every factor is centered: \[ j_i\neq j_{i+1},\qquad \tau(x_i)=0. \] We call \(a\) and \(b\) free when the unital algebras they generate are free. The condition therefore applies to centered polynomials in \(a\) and \(b\), not merely to the original variables.

Why center? Because every factor can be split into its mean and its fluctuation. Expanding these splits reduces mixed moments to simpler ones until the alternating-centered rule applies. This recursively determines the joint moments from the two marginal moment sequences.

A fourth-moment calculation

Suppose \(a,b\) are centered and free, with \[ \tau(a^2)=\alpha,\qquad \tau(b^2)=\beta. \] The definition immediately gives \[ \tau(ab)=0,\qquad \tau(abab)=0. \] But \(a^2b^2\) is different. Center the squares: \[ 0=\tau\bigl((a^2-\alpha1)(b^2-\beta1)\bigr) =\tau(a^2b^2)-\alpha\beta. \] Consequently, \[ \boxed{\tau(a^2b^2)=\alpha\beta,\qquad \tau(abab)=0.} \] For centered, classically independent scalar variables \(X,Y\) with those variances, both corresponding expressions equal \(\alpha\beta\), since \(XYXY=X^2Y^2\).

At second order, both theories give variance addition. At fourth order, the distinction becomes visible: \[ \tau((a+b)^4)=\tau(a^4)+\tau(b^4)+4\alpha\beta. \] To see the coefficient four, list the six words containing two of each letter. The words \(aabb,abba,baab,bbaa\) each contribute \(\alpha\beta\). The alternating words \(abab,baba\) vanish. Words with three of one letter and one of the other also vanish, by centering and cyclicity.

Classical independence instead gives a coefficient of six.

Adding two free sign variables

Let both marginal laws be \[ \mu=\tfrac12\delta_{-1}+\tfrac12\delta_1. \] Each variable is centered, with second and fourth moments equal to one. The free sum therefore has \[ m_2=2,\qquad m_4=6. \] The classical sum has masses \(1/4,1/2,1/4\) at \(-2,0,2\), and fourth moment eight. Perfect alignment, where \(b=a\), gives fourth moment sixteen.

We write the law of a free sum as \(\mu\boxplus\nu\), the additive free convolution. Part III will calculate the entire law in this example. Part IV will compare all three eigenbasis choices numerically.

Asymptotic freeness of random matrices

One sufficient setting is that \(D_n,E_n\) are deterministic Hermitian matrices with uniformly bounded operator norms and convergent empirical spectral measures. If \(U_n\) is Haar unitary, then the pair \((D_n,U_nE_nU_n^*)\) converges in joint normalized-trace moments to a free pair, in probability. This is a standard asymptotic-freeness theorem, not something implied just by our definition. Speicher, Section 7.

At each finite dimension, the pair generally fails exact freeness. The theorem describes the limit for each fixed word. Independent random diagonal sign matrices illustrate why ordinary independence alone is insufficient: they commute, and their alternating fourth moment is identically one.

Exercises

  1. Compute \(\tau((a+b)^2)\) for centered free variables of variances \(\alpha,\beta\).
  2. For the symmetric two-point laws above, compute \(\tau((a+b)^3)\).
  3. Explain why the identity \(a^2=b^2=1\) is compatible with \(\tau(abab)=0\).
Answer sketches
  1. The cross terms vanish, leaving \(\alpha+\beta\).
  2. It is zero. The pure cubic moments vanish by symmetry, and the mixed terms vanish by freeness and cyclicity.
  3. The squares only simplify adjacent equal factors. Simplifying \(abab\) to \(a^2b^2\) would require commutativity.
